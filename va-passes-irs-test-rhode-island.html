Worker classification — Rhode Island & Massachusetts

Your VA Passes the IRS Test. Does It Pass Rhode Island's?

Short answer: Rhode Island and Massachusetts both apply a strict three-part ABC test on top of the federal IRS test, and a worker must pass all three parts — not just the total picture — or they're an employee by default under state law. Behavioral health and medical practices are especially exposed because front-office VA work like scheduling, intake, and billing usually fails the "outside the usual course of business" prong.

Passing One Test Doesn't Mean You Pass Both

Most guidance on 1099 vs. W-2 — including a lot of what's on Reddit and Google — only covers the federal IRS test. In Massachusetts and Rhode Island, that's only half the picture. The federal common-law test weighs behavioral control, financial control, and relationship type together, with no single factor deciding it. The state ABC test is stricter: every worker is presumed an employee until the employer proves all three parts. The federal test governs payroll tax treatment; the state test governs wage law, unemployment insurance, and workers' comp — independently of the IRS.

The ABC Test, Prong by Prong

Unlike the federal test, all three prongs must be satisfied — fail one, and the worker is an employee by default.

A

Free From Control

Is the worker free from your direction over how, when, and where the work gets done — both on paper and in actual practice? This mirrors the federal behavioral-control factor.

Weighs against passing

  • You set specific hours or require a fixed schedule
  • You provide step-by-step instructions on how tasks are done
  • You supervise the work directly, day to day

Supports passing

  • Worker sets their own hours and workflow
  • You describe the outcome; they determine the method
  • Worker uses their own tools, systems, or subcontractors

Note: this prong alone is rarely where healthcare VAs fail — Prong B, next, is usually the real obstacle.

B

Outside Your Usual Business

The worker's service must be outside the usual course of your business. For a behavioral health or medical practice, that's a narrow needle to thread. Ask yourself: would my practice function without this role being filled by someone, in some form? Is this task part of the operational engine of patient care — scheduling, intake, billing, insurance follow-up — rather than a one-off side project?

The hard truth: scheduling, intake coordination, insurance verification, and billing support are not incidental to a medical or behavioral health practice — they're part of how the practice runs every single day. That makes Prong B genuinely difficult to satisfy for a VA doing this work, regardless of how independent the arrangement otherwise looks.

C

An Independent Trade

The worker needs a genuine, independently established business of the same nature — not just a side gig that happens to be you.

Weighs against passing

  • You are their only client, and have been for months
  • They don't market VA services to any other practices
  • No business registration, website, or other client contracts exist

Supports passing

  • Worker actively serves multiple practices concurrently
  • They operate under a registered business name or entity
  • They carry their own liability insurance and set their own rates

Note: a single-practice VA who's been with you a year rarely clears this bar — the exclusivity itself is evidence against it.

Why Behavioral Health Practices Are Exposed

  • VA support has been aggressively marketed to solo and small therapy practices as a low-overhead alternative to hiring front-desk staff — the exact role a receptionist traditionally filled
  • Intake, scheduling, insurance verification, and billing are core operational tasks in behavioral health, not peripheral ones — precisely what Prong B tests for
  • HIPAA-compliant VA vendors often provide practice-branded email addresses and direct EHR access, reinforcing integration into daily operations
  • Solo practitioners often engage one VA exclusively for 10–20+ hours a week, cutting against genuine independent-business status under Prong C
A common scenario: "I run a solo therapy practice in Providence. My VA handles scheduling, insurance verification, and intake forms, about 15 hours a week. She's 1099 and invoices me monthly. My accountant says the IRS test looks fine. Are we actually okay?" Running it through Rhode Island's ABC test: Prong A possibly passes if she truly sets her own hours. Prong B very likely fails — scheduling and intake are core to how a therapy practice runs. Prong C likely fails — 15 hrs/week for one practice, no other clients. The federal test being "fine" says nothing about Rhode Island exposure. Two out of three ABC prongs failing means employee status by default under state law.

What's at Stake

Massachusetts

  • Civil liability — unpaid wages, overtime, and lost benefits, potentially at treble (3x) damages
  • Criminal exposure — willful misclassification: up to $25,000 and a year in jail for a first offense
  • State contract bar — violators can be barred from contracting with the Commonwealth
  • Multi-agency reach — the AG's Fair Labor Division, Unemployment Assistance, and Industrial Accidents can each pursue this independently

Rhode Island

  • Full burden on the employer — the worker is presumed an employee from the start
  • Civil penalties — RI Dept. of Labor and Training can pursue penalties once investigated
  • Criminal referral — the Attorney General's Office can bring charges where misclassification is established
  • Independent agencies — unemployment insurance and workers' comp are assessed separately from any federal resolution

Sources: RI Dept. of Labor & Training; Mass.gov Attorney General's Fair Labor Division. General education only — confirm specifics with counsel.

Yes — and It Happens Constantly

A worker can be a legitimate federal 1099 contractor and still be a state-law employee in RI or MA — at the same time, for the same role. Passing both is the cleanest position. Passing federal but failing state is the trap: federally fine, but a state-law employee for wage, unemployment, and workers' comp purposes. Failing both means the highest exposure across every agency at once.

Does This Still Apply If Your VA Is Remote?

Many VAs supporting RI/MA practices work from elsewhere entirely — another state, or overseas. That doesn't automatically remove state exposure. A practice based in RI or MA is generally still subject to that state's wage, unemployment, and workers' comp laws for work it directs and controls, regardless of where the worker sits. The worker's own state or country may separately impose its own rules on the same relationship. Foreign-based VAs generally fall outside U.S. state wage law, but this doesn't resolve federal tax reporting questions. "My VA doesn't live in Rhode Island" is not, by itself, a reason to assume Rhode Island law doesn't apply.

What Actually Helps Your Position

  • Evidence the VA actively serves other practices — visible concurrent work, not just references
  • A registered business name, its own website or listing, and its own liability insurance
  • A written agreement describing outcomes and deliverables, not hours or supervised tasks
  • Billing at a project or deliverable rate, not an hourly rate resembling a wage
  • No practice-branded email address, and no mandatory use of internal systems where avoidable

Reality check: for most solo-practice VA arrangements handling core operational tasks, Prong B remains genuinely difficult to satisfy — documentation helps at the margins, but doesn't change the nature of the work itself.

Does it matter if my VA works remotely from another state or country?
